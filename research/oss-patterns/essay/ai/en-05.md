# The Keys We Can't Afford: Young Adults and the Housing Squeeze

There's a particular kind of silence that falls over a group of thirty-somethings when someone mentions they just bought a house. It isn't jealousy, exactly. It's closer to curiosity mixed with disbelief. The next question is almost always some version of *how?* The answer is often an inheritance, a parent's help with the down payment, or a move to a town two hours from where they actually work.

For a growing share of young adults, owning a home no longer feels like a milestone they're heading toward. It feels like a lottery they never entered.

## A Ladder With Missing Rungs

The traditional path was simple enough to sketch on a napkin. You finish school, find steady work, rent a modest apartment, save for a few years, then buy a starter home. As your income grew, you traded up. The house built equity, the equity built security, and that security was the foundation for everything else: a family, a business, a retirement.

That ladder still exists, but several rungs have gone missing. In many cities, home prices have risen far faster than wages over the past two decades. Rents have climbed so high that "saving for a few years" has become saving for a decade or more. Meanwhile, the starter home (the small, unglamorous house that used to be a first step) has nearly disappeared from new construction, because larger homes earn developers more and zoning rules often make small ones hard to build at all.

The result is a generation that's doing what it was told to do, often with more education and more credentials than their parents had, and still finding the door locked.

## It's Not About Avocado Toast

Every few years, someone resurfaces the idea that young people could afford homes if they cut back on lattes, streaming subscriptions, or brunch. The arithmetic has never worked. When a down payment in a mid-sized city runs into the tens of thousands of dollars, skipping coffee buys you a few square feet of carpet.

The real pressures are structural. Student debt eats into the money people could otherwise save. Wages for early-career workers have been unsteady, and gig and contract work has made income less predictable, which lenders notice. Interest rates have moved sharply in recent years, and even small changes can add hundreds of dollars to a monthly mortgage payment. Most of all, there simply aren't enough homes. Decades of underbuilding, restrictive land-use rules, and local opposition to new development have left many regions short of the housing their populations need.

None of that has anything to do with breakfast.

## The Ripple Effects

Housing affordability rarely stays a housing problem. When young adults can't find stable, affordable homes, the effects spread into nearly every part of life.

People delay marriage and children, or decide against them. They stay in jobs they've outgrown because moving to a better opportunity means paying a higher rent. They live with their parents longer, which can be a fine arrangement but often isn't a choice. Some leave the cities where their careers would flourish, and the cities lose the energy, talent, and tax base that young residents bring.

There's also a quieter cost: the widening gap between those who get family help and those who don't. When the down payment comes from the "Bank of Mom and Dad," homeownership starts to depend less on what you've earned and more on what you were born into. That's a troubling shift for societies that like to describe themselves as meritocracies.

## What Would Actually Help

The encouraging part is that the causes are largely known, and so are many of the remedies.

**Build more, and build differently.** Reforming zoning to allow duplexes, townhouses, accessory dwelling units, and mid-rise apartments near transit and jobs would increase supply where it's needed most. Speeding up approvals and cutting permitting delays matters too.

**Bring back the starter home.** Policies that make small homes on small lots legal and profitable to build would restore that missing first rung.

**Support renters, not just buyers.** Ownership isn't the only path to stability. Stronger tenant protections, more purpose-built rental housing, and expanded social and nonprofit housing can give young adults security without a mortgage.

**Target help carefully.** Down-payment assistance and first-time buyer programs can help, but if they aren't paired with more supply, they risk simply pushing prices higher.

## A Shared Stake

It's easy to treat this as a young person's problem. It isn't. Every community depends on teachers, nurses, tradespeople, and new entrepreneurs who need somewhere to live. Every aging homeowner will one day need buyers for their property and workers to staff the services they depend on.

Housing is where a society decides who gets to belong. Right now, too many young adults are being told, in effect, that there isn't room. We can choose to make room. The question is whether we'll do it before an entire generation stops expecting us to.

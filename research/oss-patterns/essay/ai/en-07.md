# The Sea Is Coming: How Coastal Cities Learn to Live With Water

For most of history, coastal cities treated the sea as something to hold back. The Dutch built dikes and New Orleans built levees. The idea was that a high enough wall, built strongly enough, would keep the city dry for good.

That idea is failing. Sea levels are rising, storms are getting stronger, and rain now falls in heavier bursts. A wall can always be overtopped, and when it is, the city behind it has usually done nothing else to prepare. The cities that are adapting best have stopped asking how to keep water out. They ask how to live with it.

## The limits of the wall

Hard defenses still matter. London's Thames Barrier has protected the city for four decades. Venice's MOSE gates have spared St. Mark's Square from several serious floods since they started working in 2020. But both projects show the same weakness. They are very expensive, they take decades to build, and they were designed for a climate that no longer exists. The Thames Barrier now closes far more often than its planners expected. MOSE was delayed for years and went far over budget, and it already faces questions about how long its design will hold up.

A wall also makes people feel safe, and that can be risky. Development crowds in behind the barrier, property values climb, and the losses grow when the barrier finally fails. Engineers call this the "levee effect." Hurricane Katrina showed in 2005 what it looks like when it goes wrong.

## Making room for water

The more interesting work today is softer, more spread out, and often cheaper.

Rotterdam, a city that sits largely below sea level, is the best-known example. Its "water squares" are public plazas that work as basketball courts and gathering spaces in dry weather and fill up as storage basins during heavy rain. Parking garages hold water underground, and green roofs slow the runoff. Rotterdam doesn't rely on one barrier. It treats the whole city as a sponge.

Copenhagen took a similar route after a cloudburst in 2011 flooded basements across the city and caused hundreds of millions of euros in damage. Its Cloudburst Management Plan turns streets into channels and parks into retention ponds. In a severe storm, the water is steered on purpose toward places where it can do little harm.

China has rolled out a similar idea on a national scale through its "sponge city" program. It sets targets for how much rainfall cities must absorb, using permeable pavement, wetlands, and restored riverbanks.

## Nature as infrastructure

Some of the best protection is alive. Mangrove forests, salt marshes, oyster reefs, and dunes take energy out of waves before they reach the shore. Unlike concrete, they can grow as the sea rises, building up sediment over time. They also store carbon, support fisheries, and give people somewhere to go outdoors.

New York learned this after Hurricane Sandy in 2012. Sandy flooded subway tunnels, knocked out power to lower Manhattan, and caused roughly $19 billion in damage to the city. One response, the "Living Breakwaters" project off Staten Island, uses reef-like structures seeded with oysters to break waves and restore habitat at the same time. It isn't a substitute for engineering. It is engineering that does more than one job.

## The hardest conversation: retreat

Not every place can be saved, and adaptation sometimes means admitting that. "Managed retreat" means planned, funded relocation away from the most exposed land, and it is politically toxic almost everywhere. Homeowners don't want to leave, local governments don't want to lose their tax base, and nobody wants to be the official who tells a neighborhood it has no future.

Yet retreat is already happening, mostly without a plan. Insurers are pulling out of high-risk markets in Florida and elsewhere. Buyout programs have moved some flood-prone households in New Jersey and North Carolina. At the extreme end, Indonesia is building a new capital on Borneo, partly because large parts of Jakarta are sinking from groundwater extraction even as the sea rises. When retreat comes without a plan, it is done by price tags and disasters, and it falls hardest on people who can't afford to move first.

## Equity is the test

That points to the central challenge. Adaptation can protect the vulnerable or it can push them out. A new waterfront park with flood berms raises nearby property values, and the residents it was meant to protect may soon find they can't afford to live there. Researchers call this "climate gentrification," and it is already visible in places like Miami, where higher-elevation neighborhoods that were long home to working-class communities are attracting new investment.

The cities that get adaptation right will judge success by who stays safe, not only by how dry the streets stay.

## A different relationship with the coast

Coastal cities exist because the sea brought trade, food, and connection. That relationship isn't ending, but it is changing. The cities that do well this century will see water as a permanent neighbor to plan around. They will design streets that can flood, restore the marshes earlier generations filled in, and talk honestly about where people should and shouldn't live.

The sea is coming either way. What each city controls is whether it is ready when the water arrives.

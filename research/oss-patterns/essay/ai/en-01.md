# The University After the Answer Machine

For most of modern history, universities did well because knowledge was scarce. Libraries held books that most people couldn't get, professors had expertise most people couldn't reach, and a degree showed that you had spent years getting access to both. Generative AI hasn't ended that arrangement, but it has made one of its main assumptions false: competent answers to most questions are no longer scarce. They are close to free.

That fact should change how universities think about teaching, assessment and their own purpose. Most institutions have so far treated AI as a discipline problem. They write honor-code addenda, buy detection software and run anxious faculty workshops on "catching" students. That is the wrong frame. The question isn't how to stop students using these tools. It's what a university is for once they exist.

## 1. Stop grading the artifact, start grading the thinking

The take-home essay, the problem set and the lab report all assumed that the finished product was good evidence of the thinking behind it. That assumption has collapsed. A polished five-page essay now shows only that someone had access to a chatbot and a few minutes.

Universities should move their assessment weight toward things that reveal reasoning as it happens. Oral examinations, once common and now rare outside doctoral defenses, deserve a comeback. So do in-class writing, structured debates, critiques of drafts and portfolios that show how a project changed over a semester. An instructor who asks "Why did you abandon your first thesis?" learns more in two minutes than a plagiarism detector learns in a thousand scans.

This costs more in faculty time, and that is the point. Cheap assessment was always a compromise. AI has made that compromise impossible to keep.

## 2. Teach AI fluency as a core literacy, not an elective

A graduate who can't work well with AI tools in 2026 is in roughly the position of a graduate who couldn't use a search engine in 2005. Fluency doesn't mean knowing how to type a prompt, though. It means knowing when a model is likely to be confidently wrong. It means checking a citation, noticing when an output reflects bias in its training data and judging when a task shouldn't be handed off at all.

Universities should build this into every discipline instead of leaving it to one optional course in the computer science department. A history student should learn how AI flattens historiographical debate. A nursing student should learn where clinical decision tools fail. A law student should learn why invented case citations have already gotten practicing attorneys sanctioned. In each case, the critical skill belongs to the discipline itself.

## 3. Protect the struggle

There is a real risk on the other side, and enthusiasts tend to skip it. Learning often depends on productive difficulty: the frustration of a proof that won't close, or a paragraph that won't say what you mean. If a tool removes every obstacle as soon as it appears, students may produce more and learn less.

So universities must also say clearly where AI does not belong. Introductory courses that build foundational skills like writing, quantitative reasoning and reading difficult texts may need protected spaces where students work without help, the way a gym bans forklifts. The rule shouldn't be "AI is banned." It should be "this exercise exists to build a muscle, and the tool would do the lifting for you." Students accept constraints far more readily when the reason is explained instead of imposed.

## 4. Redefine what faculty are for

If lectures that only pass on information compete with an endlessly patient tutor available at 3 a.m., the lecture as a delivery mechanism is in trouble. That doesn't make professors less valuable. They become valuable for different reasons: mentorship, judgment, modeling how an expert thinks through ambiguity, and building communities of inquiry.

Institutions should reward this shift in hiring, promotion and workload policies. A professor who redesigns a course around discussion and individual feedback is doing harder work than one who reuses old lecture slides. Tenure committees should recognize that.

## 5. Be honest about the value proposition

Finally, universities need to face the question that students and parents are already asking: what am I paying for? If the answer is "information," AI makes the price look absurd. The better answer has always been there, even if it was buried under credentialism. A university offers a community of people who think seriously together, access to mentors who know you by name, practice at doing hard things alongside others, and the chance to make mistakes when the stakes are still low.

Generative AI didn't create the gap between that ideal and the reality of large lectures and multiple-choice exams. It exposed it. The universities that do well in the coming decade will treat this as a chance to become what they always claimed to be, not as a threat to manage.

The answer machine is here. The schools that matter will be the ones that teach students how to ask better questions.

# The Great Squeeze: What the Streaming Wars Mean for Independent Film

A decade ago, the pitch was irresistible. Streaming platforms, flush with subscriber-growth cash and hungry for content, would democratize film. The gatekeepers of the old studio system would loosen their grip, and a thousand idiosyncratic voices would finally find an audience. For a few golden years, it looked true. Today, the picture is more complicated, and for independent filmmakers, more precarious.

## The Gold Rush and the Hangover

Between roughly 2015 and 2021, streamers behaved like prospectors. Netflix, Amazon, Apple, Hulu and a parade of newcomers bought festival films at prices that made sellers dizzy. A small drama that might once have fetched a modest theatrical distribution deal could suddenly command eight figures at Sundance. Producers learned to love the "buyout": a flat fee, paid up front, that made a film profitable before a single viewer pressed play.

That certainty came with a hidden cost. Buyouts meant filmmakers surrendered the upside. If a film became a phenomenon, the platform kept the windfall. Worse, the data stayed locked in a black box. Creators were rarely told how many people watched their work, which made it nearly impossible to build a career on proof of audience.

Then the money tightened. Wall Street stopped rewarding subscriber growth at any cost and began demanding profit. Platforms consolidated, canceled projects, and in some cases pulled finished films from their libraries for tax write-offs. The festival marketplace cooled. Acquisition budgets shrank, and many films that would have been snapped up in 2019 now leave Park City without a buyer.

## The Middle Is Disappearing

The most worrying trend is the hollowing out of the middle. Studios and streamers increasingly concentrate their spending on two poles: massive franchise tentpoles and cheap, algorithm-friendly content. The mid-budget film, the adult drama or sharp comedy costing five to thirty million dollars, is the casualty. It is too expensive to be a gamble and too modest to be a brand.

Independent film has always lived in that middle, or just below it. When the middle thins, the ladder breaks. Directors who once moved from a micro-budget debut to a mid-sized project now face a gap with no rungs. Many either stay small indefinitely or get absorbed into franchise work, where their distinct sensibilities are sanded down.

## Discovery Is the Real Battleground

Here's the paradox: there has never been more access to film, and never been a harder time being found. A subscriber facing ten thousand titles tends to default to what's familiar, or what the interface pushes. Algorithms favor recognizable stars, existing franchises and content that keeps people watching rather than content that makes them think.

An independent film doesn't just need distribution; it needs attention. The old theatrical release, imperfect as it was, created a cultural moment: reviews, conversations, awards buzz. A film dropped quietly into a streaming library often vanishes within a week, no matter its quality.

## Reasons for Cautious Optimism

It would be too bleak to stop there. The turbulence has opened some doors.

**Theatrical is wobbling back as a mark of prestige.** Even streamers now court limited theatrical runs to qualify for awards and generate buzz. Boutique distributors and specialty chains have emerged to serve audiences who want curation over infinite scroll.

**Niche platforms are thriving.** Services built around specific tastes, such as arthouse, horror, international cinema and classic film, offer filmmakers a home where the audience is already looking for something like their work. These platforms pay less, but they pay with attention.

**Direct-to-audience tools are maturing.** Creators can now sell or rent films directly, build mailing lists, and finance projects through crowdfunding and community screenings. It's harder work, but it returns ownership and, crucially, audience data.

**Free ad-supported streaming offers a second life.** Libraries of older independent films are finding new viewers on free tiers, giving catalog titles a long tail they never had before.

## What Filmmakers Should Do

The lesson of the streaming wars is that no single platform should be your destiny. Retain rights wherever possible. Negotiate for performance data and back-end bonuses. Build an audience before you need one. And treat festivals not just as sales venues but as launchpads for relationships with critics, programmers and fellow artists.

## The Long View

Independent film has survived the death of the studio contract player, the rise of home video, the collapse of the DVD market and the multiplex takeover. It is a resilient art form precisely because it is cheap to attempt and impossible to fully control. The streaming wars didn't kill it, and they didn't save it either. They simply rearranged the terrain.

The filmmakers who thrive will be those who treat distribution as part of the creative act, who understand that finding an audience is a craft just like cinematography or editing. The gold rush is over. What comes next may be smaller, scrappier and, if we are lucky, more honest.

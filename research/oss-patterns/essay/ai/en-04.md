# The Quiet Revolution on Two and Three Wheels: EVs in Emerging Markets

When Western commentators talk about the electric vehicle transition, they usually picture a sleek sedan, a home charger, and a generous government rebate. That picture describes a minority of the world's drivers. The more consequential story is unfolding in Jakarta, Lagos, Hanoi, Nairobi, and Bengaluru, where the future of mobility is being built on a different template entirely.

## Start Small, Scale Fast

In most emerging markets, the dominant vehicle is not the car. It is the motorcycle, the scooter, the auto-rickshaw, and the minibus. These vehicles carry the working lives of hundreds of millions of people, and they are the ideal candidates for electrification.

The economics are persuasive. A small electric two-wheeler has a modest battery, so its upfront premium is far smaller than that of an electric car. Its running costs are dramatically lower: electricity per kilometer costs a fraction of petrol, and electric motors have few moving parts to repair. For a delivery rider or ride-hailing driver who covers 100 kilometers a day, savings can amount to several weeks of income each year. This is not a lifestyle choice or a climate gesture. It is a raise.

India, Vietnam, and parts of Southeast Asia have already seen electric two- and three-wheelers capture meaningful market share. Electric buses are following, with cities in Latin America, notably Santiago and Bogotá, running some of the largest electric fleets outside China.

## The Chinese Factor

No discussion of this topic is honest without acknowledging China. Chinese manufacturers dominate battery production and have driven down costs relentlessly. Their exports of affordable electric cars, scooters, and buses are reshaping what is possible in price-sensitive markets. Models priced at levels once unthinkable for an EV now appear in showrooms from Brazil to Thailand to Egypt.

This brings opportunity and tension. Consumers benefit from cheaper vehicles. But governments want more than imports; they want factories, jobs, and technology transfer. Indonesia, with its vast nickel reserves, has used export restrictions and incentives to lure battery investment. India has pursued production-linked subsidies. Brazil and Mexico are weighing tariffs against the desire for cheaper cars. The countries that negotiate well may climb the value chain; those that simply import will remain customers.

## The Grid and the Garage

The obstacles are real. Charging infrastructure is thin, and many households lack a private garage or even a reliable outlet. Electricity grids in some countries suffer outages that make charging a gamble. And where grids rely heavily on coal, the climate benefit of an EV is smaller, though still usually positive over the vehicle's lifetime and growing as grids decarbonize.

Yet emerging markets are inventing solutions that wealthy countries have barely considered. **Battery swapping** is the standout. Instead of waiting an hour to charge, a rider pulls into a station, exchanges a depleted battery for a full one in under two minutes, and leaves. Companies in India, Kenya, Nigeria, and Rwanda are scaling this model, and it suits the dense, high-mileage use of commercial two-wheelers. Swapping also decouples the battery from the vehicle, lowering the sticker price and allowing the battery to be financed as a service.

Solar offers another path. Distributed solar paired with storage can power charging hubs where the grid is weak, turning a liability into an advantage. Several African startups are already building such systems.

## Finance Is the Real Bottleneck

Ask operators across these markets what holds back adoption, and the answer is rarely the technology. It is capital. Lenders unfamiliar with EVs hesitate to finance them, uncertain about resale values and battery lifespans. Interest rates are high, and a vehicle with a higher upfront cost but lower running cost is penalized by short-term thinking.

The fix is partly institutional. Development banks can guarantee loans, standardize battery health data, and support second-life markets for used packs. Pay-as-you-go models, borrowed from mobile phones and solar home systems, are helping riders spread costs over time. When financing catches up with the economics, adoption tends to accelerate sharply.

## What to Expect

Over the next decade, I expect emerging markets to lead the world in electrified vehicle *count*, even if wealthy countries lead in electric *cars*. The transition will look messy and uneven: swapping stations beside petrol pumps, Chinese hatchbacks sharing roads with homegrown electric rickshaws, policy swinging between ambition and caution.

It will also matter enormously. Transport emissions and urban air pollution are most punishing in fast-growing cities. Leapfrogging directly to electric mobility, much as many countries skipped landlines for mobile phones, could spare millions of people from the worst of both. The revolution will not arrive in a luxury sedan. It will arrive on a scooter, carrying a delivery bag, quietly saving its rider money every single day.
